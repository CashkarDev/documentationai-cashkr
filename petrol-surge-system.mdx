<Callout kind="warning">

**This is a design proposal. Nothing described here is built.**

No part of the Petrol Surge System exists in the product today. Every rupee figure below is a placeholder scaled to market benchmarks rather than to Cashkr's own margins, and the premise the whole design rests on — that partners refuse orders because of travel cost — is still an assumption. Treat this page as a design on record, not as product behaviour.

</Callout>

## Overview

A Cashkr pickup can be 15 km from the partner's base. Quick-commerce platforms operate on 1.5–3 km radii, so their distance pay is a small component; for Cashkr it is not. The proposal is to pay the partner a bonus on trips that do not pay for themselves, so that far and low-value pickups still get accepted.

The system has two layers. A **deterministic trip allowance** is published and predictable, so partners can trust it. A **dynamic surge** reacts to starvation, weather and time, is capped, and is never shown as a ladder.

## Before anything is built

Three questions gate the entire design. None are engineering problems, and two change the numbers materially.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Does the rejection history exist at all?" icon="database">

  The system is fed by one event stream — offer shown → accepted, rejected or expired → reassigned. It is unresolved whether per-transition timestamps are stored, or whether the order status column is simply overwritten.

  If it is overwritten, that history does not exist and every number in this page is unbuildable until it does.

  **Owner:** Engineering — check the order status resolver.

  </Step>

  <Step title="What is gross margin per completed order, by device band?" icon="calculator">

  Every cap in the calculation is currently anchored to what food and quick-commerce platforms pay, not to what a Cashkr pickup earns. A ₹250 absolute cap is either conservative or ruinous depending on a number nobody has put in front of this design.

  **Owner:** Finance.

  </Step>

  <Step title="Does a travel bonus enter the 5% gig-worker contribution base?" icon="scale">

  The Social Security (Central) Rules 2026 took effect 8 May 2026. Aggregators contribute a notified percentage of turnover, or 5% of amounts paid or payable to gig workers. Whether a travel bonus sits inside that base is a legal reading, not an engineering assumption — and it is the difference between surge costing X and 1.05X.

  **Owner:** Yogesh.

  </Step>
</Steps>

## The premise — it is not petrol, it is time

At Mumbai's pump price of ₹111.21/litre and a realistic 45 km/l on a 110cc commuter, fuel costs about ₹2.47/km. A 16 km round trip burns roughly ₹40 of petrol.

But a Cashkr pickup is not a drop-and-go. The Partners app flow runs guided inspection with photo capture, a requote step requiring customer approval, payment, and a digital signature — 45 to 75 minutes on site, on top of travel. The agent's real cost on that trip is closer to ₹120–170, of which petrol is about a quarter.

So the thing being compensated is the trip, not the fuel. The design models it as trip compensation internally and calls it a petrol bonus in the partner app, because *petrol ka kharcha* is the language an agent actually uses and it reads as transparent rather than algorithmic. The internal and partner-facing names differ deliberately.

<Callout kind="warning">

**Do not skip to the calculation.** Production figures show roughly 270 orders/day arriving against about 39/day completing, in the 60-day window to June 2026. A large share of that gap is customers dropping off at quote stage (`isCompleted=0`), not partners refusing trips.

Size the partner-refusal slice before spending a rupee on surge. It may be a small fraction of a gap that surge cannot touch.

</Callout>

## Cost model

| Component | Mumbai, Sep 2026 | Basis | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Petrol | ₹111.21/l | Pump price, 22 Sep | Verified |
| Two-wheeler fuel | ₹2.22–2.78/km | 40–50 km/l | Verified |
| Diesel — car pickups, laptops | ₹97.83/l → ₹6.52/km | 15 km/l | Verified |
| Wear, tyres, service | ~₹1.00/km | Estimate, unsourced | Inferred |
| Agent time | ~₹2.50/km-equivalent | From ₹100/hr opportunity cost | Inferred |
| **All-in, two-wheeler** | **~₹6/km** | Sum of the above | Inferred |

That lands on the same number as the market, which pays ₹5–10/km distance pay beyond a free radius. Convergent from two directions, which makes ₹6/km a defensible starting rate even though half its components are estimates.

### What the market pays

The structural pattern is consistent across platforms: base pay ₹15–35 per order, distance pay ₹5–10/km beyond a free radius, and a surge layer of ₹10–20 per order for rain or demand spikes. Zepto's rain and high-demand bonus sits at ₹10–20; Zomato brands its version "rain surge". No platform in that set offers a fuel allowance as a named line — it is buried in distance pay.

<Callout kind="info">

**Who is publishing these numbers matters.** The rider-earnings figures circulating for 2026 come from gig referral and job-aggregation sites that earn on rider sign-ups, so their headline monthlies are advertised ceilings, not medians. PickMyWork is at least explicit about the split: realistic take-home of ₹15,000–30,000 in metros and ₹10,000–15,000 in Tier 2, with fuel and maintenance borne entirely by the partner.

</Callout>

A better anchor than rider pay is the **Motor Vehicles Aggregator Guidelines 2025**, which cap surge at 2× base fare (raised from 1.5×), set a floor of 50% of base, and mandate that drivers receive at least 80% of the fare. That is the Indian regulator's stated view of a defensible surge. It does not bind Cashkr legally, but it is a sensible self-imposed ceiling and a good answer to have ready.

## Triggers

Six triggers, each with its own enable flag so one can be killed without killing surge.

| Trigger | Fires on | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distance band | Round-trip km, partner service-area centroid → pickup pincode centroid | Deliberately **not live GPS**. Vendor location awareness is tier-1 only today. Centroids are also far harder to spoof. |
| Low order value | Bonus-to-quote ratio, not distance alone | The one that protects margin. A ₹1,200 feature phone 12 km out is never worth a trip to anyone. |
| Time of day | Evening slots, weekends | — |
| Weather | Rain | Mumbai monsoon. This is where a rain surge earns its keep. |
| Zone starvation | Unassigned for T minutes, or rejected N times | Most powerful and most dangerous — it finds the clearing price without anyone guessing it, and teaches partners that refusing pays more. See abuse controls. |
| Slot-breach risk | Order approaching its committed window | — |

## Calculation

```js
// Layer 1 — deterministic trip allowance (published, builds trust)
trip_allowance = round_to_5(
      max(0, round_trip_km - FREE_KM) * RATE_PER_KM
    + DWELL_ALLOWANCE[device_category]
)
// FREE_KM = 6 (round trip), RATE_PER_KM = 6
// DWELL_ALLOWANCE: phone 0, tablet 20, laptop 40

// Layer 2 — dynamic surge (reactive, capped, never shown as a ladder)
surge = min(
      SURGE_CAP,
      STEP * escalation_level * zone_multiplier * weather_multiplier
)
// STEP = 20; escalation_level increments every 8 min unassigned
// or per rejection; capped at 3

pickup_bonus = min(
      trip_allowance + surge,
      CAP_ABS,                    // e.g. ₹250
      CAP_PCT * quote_value       // e.g. 12% of the customer quote
)
```

**The percentage cap is the one people forget.** Without it the system will one day pay ₹180 of bonus on a ₹900 device, and nobody finds out until month-end.

### Surge needs an exit

If `pickup_bonus` reaches the cap and the order is still unclaimed, the system must stop bidding and route elsewhere — batch it with another pickup in that pincode, push the customer to a later slot with a small quote sweetener, or offer a drop-off at a partner store.

Most surge systems have no exit condition and quietly become an uncapped subsidy. This one should be able to say *no bid at cap* and hand the order to a different fulfilment path.

## Funding options

| Option | How it works | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A — Direct wallet credit** | Cashkr posts the bonus into the partner's ledger on completion. | Clean, visible to the agent, straight P&L cost. The per-partner ledger on the pooled HDFC account already supports it. Waits on the rupee-denominated wallet. |
| **B — Lead-fee waiver** | Reduce or waive the credits charged to the partner for that order. | Costs foregone revenue rather than cash out, needs zero payment integration, ships quickly. But for a partner who is a shop employing riders, the money never reaches the person on the bike. |
| **C — Hybrid** *(recommended)* | B for the pilot, A as the target once the rupee wallet lands, with a rule that partner-businesses must surface the bonus to their agent in the app. | Learns price elasticity now on the credits system without waiting on the HDFC migration, and respects the zero-downtime constraint. |

Option B's flaw matters more over time: the partner growth path actively markets "start solo, then hire your own pickup agents", so an increasing share of trips is done by someone who is not the wallet holder.

## Data model

```prisma
model SurgeRule {
  id             String   @id @default(cuid())
  cityId         String
  zoneId         String?
  version        Int
  freeKm         Decimal
  ratePerKm      Decimal
  stepAmount     Decimal
  maxEscalation  Int
  capAbs         Decimal
  capPctOfQuote  Decimal
  triggers       Json     // per-trigger enable flags + params
  effectiveFrom  DateTime
  effectiveTo    DateTime?
  createdBy      String
  @@index([cityId, zoneId, effectiveFrom])
}

model SurgeBudget {
  id            String   @id @default(cuid())
  cityId        String
  periodDate    DateTime @db.Date
  dailyCapPaise BigInt
  consumedPaise BigInt   @default(0)
  @@unique([cityId, periodDate])
}

model OrderSurgeQuote {
  id               String   @id @default(cuid())
  orderId          String
  ruleVersion      Int
  inputsSnapshot   Json     // km, quote value, weather, time, escalation
  tripAllowance    Decimal
  surgeAmount      Decimal
  totalBonus       Decimal
  escalationLevel  Int
  state            SurgeState  // SHADOW, OFFERED, LOCKED, SETTLED, VOID
  lockedPartnerId  String?
  lockedAt         DateTime?
  settledAt        DateTime?
  @@index([orderId, state])
}

model OrderAssignmentEvent {
  id         String   @id @default(cuid())
  orderId    String
  partnerId  String
  eventType  AssignmentEvent  // OFFERED, ACCEPTED, REJECTED, EXPIRED, CANCELLED
  bonusShown Decimal?
  reason     String?
  createdAt  DateTime @default(now())
  @@index([orderId, createdAt])
  @@index([partnerId, createdAt])
}
```

Two rules are non-negotiable:

- **Snapshot every input on the quote.** Six months from now a partner will dispute a ₹40 bonus, and the system must be able to reconstruct exactly what it saw.
- **Lock at acceptance, never recompute at settlement.** Settlement reads the locked amount. The moment a bonus can drift between accept and payout, partner trust is gone — and trust is the entire product.

Redis carries the hot path: zone escalation counters, budget consumption (atomic `INCRBY` behind a Lua script that checks the cap in the same round trip), and the rule cache. Postgres stays the source of truth.

<Callout kind="warning">

**Stack hazard.** `CashkrBackend` and `new-2026-admin-panel` are separate Prisma projects pointed at the same database and `public` schema, sharing one `_prisma_migrations` table — this is what produced the WipeStatus failure. Create all surge tables and enums from one project and mark them introspected-only in the other, or this feature reproduces that exact bug.

</Callout>

## Interfaces

- `POST /surge/evaluate` — internal, called by the offer service.
- Partner order feed carries a `pickupBonus` object per order.
- `POST /partner/orders/:id/accept` — performs the server-side lock and returns the locked amount, so the app can show a confirmed figure.
- Settlement hook on order completion posts the ledger entry.
- Admin: rule CRUD with versioning, budget config, and a global kill switch.
- Admin: **dry-run simulation** — point a candidate rule at the last 30 days of real orders and get back projected spend, orders affected and median bonus. Without it, every rule change is a live experiment on production money.

Build it as a module inside the backend behind a clean interface, not a microservice on day one. It is a good extraction candidate later — read-heavy, own config, must be independently killable — but extracting it now adds a network hop to the order-assignment critical path for no benefit.

**Fail open.** Any error in surge evaluation returns a zero bonus and lets the order flow. Surge going down must never stop a pickup being assigned.

## Partner app

The current rule is that pre-acceptance shows device model and quoted price only, not the address. That was deliberate, so the following is a proposal rather than a correction.

An agent cannot price a trip without knowing roughly how far it is, and a bonus shown with no context reads as arbitrary. The middle path is a coarse area label and a distance band, never a street address:

> iPhone 13 128GB · ₹18,400 · Andheri–Jogeshwari, 8–10 km · +₹60 petrol bonus

That is enough to decide, without enabling cherry-picking by building or going direct to the customer. If the original reason for hiding location was disintermediation risk, a band does not reopen it; if it was something else, the rule should stand and the bonus carries the signal alone.

The bonus must then appear as its own line in the earnings dashboard and the payout breakdown — *Petrol bonus ₹60* beside the order value. Hiding it inside a lump sum wastes the entire trust benefit being paid for.

<Callout kind="info">

Strings that suit this audience: *"Door ka pickup — ₹60 petrol bonus"*, *"Petrol kharcha cover: ₹60"*, *"Barish bonus ₹20 add hua"*.

Avoid the word **surge** in the partner UI entirely. It carries a price-gouging association in India and reads as something done to you, not for you.

</Callout>

## Abuse vectors and controls

| Vector | Control |
| --- | --- |
| Collusive rejection to pump the ladder | Cap escalation at three steps. Score partners on rejection rate. Show escalated offers to high-rejection partners later rather than first, and randomise the order of the escalated broadcast so refusing does not reliably win the higher offer. |
| Accept, then cancel after seeing the address | Forfeit the bonus, apply a cancellation penalty in the spirit of the MVAG rule (10% of value, capped), and count it toward the rejection score. |
| Address manipulation to inflate distance | Freeze the pincode at order creation. Any later change triggers a re-quote and resets surge to level zero. |
| Self-dealing — a relative books a far pickup to farm the bonus | Dedupe on customer phone and payout beneficiary; alert on repeat customer↔partner pairs. There are ~15.8k saved beneficiary rows to check against. |
| GPS spoofing | Largely neutralised by the pincode-centroid approach — a good reason to keep centroids even after live tracking ships. |
| Runaway spend | Hard daily cap per city, checked atomically before the offer renders. Global kill switch. Alert when any city crosses 70% of its daily budget. |

## Rollout

<Steps>
  <Step title="Phase 0 — instrumentation only" icon="database">

  Ship `OrderAssignmentEvent` as an append-only table. No surge logic, nothing paid, nothing shown.

  Two weeks of this tells you whether travel cost is actually the problem, and sizes the partner-refusal slice of the 270 → 39 gap. This phase is worth shipping regardless of whether surge follows.

  </Step>

  <Step title="Phase 1 — shadow mode" icon="eye">

  Run the full calculation for every order for two weeks. Store the bonus, pay nothing, show nothing. This produces the real budget model before a rupee is spent.

  </Step>

  <Step title="Phase 2 — one zone, deterministic only" icon="map-pin">

  Trip allowance live in a single zone, no escalation ladder, behind a per-partner flag.

  </Step>

  <Step title="Phase 3 — add the ladder" icon="trending-up">

  Escalation enabled in that same zone, with the abuse controls active from day one.

  </Step>

  <Step title="Phase 4 — expand" icon="map">

  Zone by zone. Exercise the kill switch in production at least once before widening.

  </Step>
</Steps>

All migrations are additive — new tables only, no `ALTER` on hot tables, new columns nullable with defaults. That satisfies the zero-downtime constraint without special handling.

## Measurement

<Callout kind="warning">

**Hold out 10–20% of eligible orders, permanently.** This is the single most important decision in the design. Without an unsurged control group you cannot distinguish orders surge *caused* from orders that would have completed anyway, and you will pay for the latter forever.

</Callout>

| Metric | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Completed orders/day vs ~39 baseline | The headline, but meaningless without the holdout. |
| Offer→accept rate, time to first accept | Whether the bonus is actually changing partner behaviour. |
| **Cost per incremental completed order** | Surge spend ÷ completions above the holdout baseline. **This is the number that decides whether the feature lives.** |
| Surge spend as share of gross margin | The affordability ceiling. |
| Bonus distribution, p95 not median | The median will look fine long after the tail stops being fine. |
| Accept rate on *non-surged* orders | The counter-metric. If partners learn to wait for a bonus, the baseline quietly rots — spend climbs while completions stay flat. |
